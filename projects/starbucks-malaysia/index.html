Introduction

Why Customer Perceived Value, and why Malaysia

Starbucks has long competed on more than coffee — its value proposition rests on the experience surrounding the cup. But an experience-led premium is only as strong as the customer's perception of it, and that perception shifts by market. This study examines Starbucks' existing internal and external Customer Perceived Value (CPV) specifically within Malaysia, then tests that value empirically against its leading local rival, Costa Coffee. Where the current proposition matches customer needs, and where it doesn't, a revised value proposition is proposed in favour of the present Malaysian marketing environment.

Company Background

Two chains, founded the same year, competing an ocean away

Starbucks and Costa Coffee were both founded in 1971 — Starbucks in Seattle, Costa Coffee in London — yet they arrived at very different global identities. Starbucks scaled into the world's most recognisable "third place" coffeehouse brand; Costa Coffee grew as a UK high-street mainstay before being acquired by The Coca-Cola Company in 2019, giving it fresh international distribution muscle. In Malaysia, both now compete directly for the same urban, brand-conscious coffee drinker.

Founded 1971 · Seattle

Starbucks Malaysia

Operating in Malaysia since 1998 under a master franchise, Starbucks positions itself at the premium end of the market — full "third place" store experience, Halal certification serving Malaysia's majority-Muslim consumer base, and a mature digital rewards ecosystem layered on top of the core coffeehouse format.

Founded 1971 · London

Costa Coffee Malaysia

Expanding in Malaysia through franchise partners and accelerated by Coca-Cola's global backing, Costa positions closer to accessible-premium — a slightly lower price tier, a footprint that leans into malls, petrol stations and quick-service formats, and a more transactional, less destination-led store experience.

The Framework

Theoretical Basis

What "Customer Perceived Value" actually means

Zeithaml (1988) defines perceived value as the consumer's overall assessment of a product's utility, based on perceptions of what is received against what is given. Kotler and Keller extend this into a practical model: Total Customer Value — product, service, personnel and image value — set against Total Customer Cost — monetary price, time, energy and psychological cost. Woodruff (1997) adds that perceived value is really about how well a product's attributes help the customer achieve their goals in the situation they're actually in — not the situation the brand imagines.

Kotler's Total Customer Value Model

Total Customer Value − Total Customer Cost = Customer Perceived Value

Applied to Starbucks Malaysia, this study splits CPV into two working lenses: Internal CPV — value drivers fully within Starbucks' control (product, ambience, personnel, brand) — and External CPV — value shaped by the market context the customer experiences it in (price positioning, accessibility, digital ecosystem, cultural fit).

Applying the Framework

Internal vs. External CPV — Starbucks Malaysia

Internal CPV
Product Value

Globally consistent quality standard, supplemented by regionally adapted beverages and seasonal local-flavour specials.

Service & Environment Value

The "third place" concept — store design, comfortable seating, free Wi-Fi and ambient music — positions outlets as a social or work space, not just a coffee counter.

Personnel Value

Globally standardised barista training builds consistent, personalised service — including the small ritual of calling out a customer's name.

Image Value

Premium global brand prestige and a consistent visual identity carry aspirational, social-status value for the Malaysian urban consumer.

External CPV
Price Positioning

Priced at a premium tier relative to local Malaysian cafés and QSR chains, targeting an aspirational urban middle-to-upper income segment.

Accessibility

Store network concentrated in urban malls, business districts and airports, with growing drive-thru format expansion.

Digital Ecosystem

Starbucks Rewards — mobile order & pay, the Starbucks Card, and gamified Stars — layers convenience and habit-forming loyalty on top of the physical experience.

Cultural & Social Fit

Halal certification is a critical trust signal for Malaysia's majority-Muslim consumer base; Instagram-friendly store design and seasonal cup campaigns drive social sharing among younger consumers.

Findings

Gap Analysis

Where the proposition matches — and where it doesn't

Matches
  • The digital loyalty ecosystem strongly relieves the "convenience" pain and creates habitual gain through gamified Stars.
  • The "third place" ambience directly answers the customer job of finding a comfortable place to work, study or meet.
  • Halal certification removes a religious-dietary barrier that would otherwise exclude a majority of the market outright.
Needs / Gaps
  • Price sensitivity among younger, budget-conscious Malaysian consumers is only partially addressed by the current tiering.
  • Smaller urban outlets face seating and Wi-Fi capacity strain during peak hours, undermining the "third place" promise precisely when it's most in demand.
  • Menu localisation still trails competitors who lean further into hyper-local flavour profiles beyond seasonal specials.

Proposed Revised Value Proposition

What the value proposition should say next

"To deliver Malaysia's most trusted premium coffee experience — pairing globally consistent quality and Halal-assured service with locally inspired flavours, flexible price tiers, and a seamless digital ecosystem that lets every customer choose their own place, pace and price to belong."

Marketing Recommendations

Turning the proposition into the marketing mix

Conclusion

Starbucks' internal CPV in Malaysia is strong where it has always been strong — product consistency, ambience and brand prestige — and its external CPV has matured meaningfully through Halal certification and a genuinely useful digital rewards ecosystem. The gap is not in what Starbucks does well, but in who it currently under-serves: price-sensitive younger consumers and peak-hour customers whose "third place" experience is crowded out by its own popularity. A revised value proposition built around flexible price tiers, deeper localisation and continued digital convenience closes that gap without diluting the premium positioning that differentiates Starbucks from Costa Coffee in the first place.
